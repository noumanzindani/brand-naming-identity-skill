# Copy the Agency Funnel, Skip the Overhead

The best naming and identity firms do not depend on flashes of genius. They run one disciplined funnel: research and diagnosis, a short written strategy, very wide idea generation, screening from cheap checks to expensive ones, and a decision made against criteria agreed before any options were shown. Lexicon writes about **2,000–3,000 candidate names** to find one, and roughly 90% of them die in legal, linguistic and strategic screening. Pentagram, Wolff Olins, Collins and DesignStudio spend **6–24 months** turning a brand idea into a toolkit of mark, custom type, screen-tested colour, motion and voice. Fees run **$75K–$150K for naming alone** at Lexicon and low-to-mid six figures for identity. Most of that money pays for scale: stakeholder politics, research in many markets, custom typefaces and global rollout. A Pakistan-based Flutter developer needs very little of it. The parts that carry the value can be copied for **about 7–14 working days and $0–$5K**: a three-hour brand sprint, a few hundred candidate names, knockout screening on Pakistani, US and international registers and both app stores, a human-drawn mark tested as a one-colour silhouette, and a token-driven `ColorScheme`. The steps worth paying for are trademark clearance on the final name, a skilled designer for the mark and type pairing, and native-speaker checks in Urdu and your export languages. The theme is where the brand actually reaches users. It should be built from tokens, not hand-tuned hex values, and contrast-checked in both light and dark mode.

## Every top firm writes the strategy before generating a single name

All of these firms spend the first phase deciding what the brand must do, not making things. Alina Wheeler's textbook process, the closest thing the field has to a standard, starts with research: business understanding, market research, then marketing, competitive, stakeholder and language audits, ending in an "audit readout." Only after that does strategy narrow to a "big idea" and a **brand brief** that feeds design ([Designing Brand Identity TOC](https://toc.uni.li/FLMF039378.pdf); [Logo Design Love](https://www.logodesignlove.com/brand-identity-process)). Mark Ritson states the same order as an axiom, "diagnosis first, strategy second," and adds that "strategy is choosing what you will not do" ([MiniMBA](https://minimba.com/articles/three-axioms-brand-strategy)). The naming specialists follow it too. Lexicon's "diamond" defines what the company must win, what it has, what it needs and what it must say *before* anyone searches for words ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek)). Igor's first step is deciding what the name must do for marketing, which narrows the search "to a certain category of name" ([Igor](https://igorinternational.com/process/name-development-product-company.php)).

The workshop tools that turn research into choices are well documented and cost nothing. **Personality sliders** pair opposites such as "Innovative ↔ Traditional." Each participant marks them privately first, so disagreements show where the strategy is still undecided, and plotting competitors on the same sliders produces a positioning map ([Learning Loop](https://learningloop.io/plays/workshop-exercise/personality-sliders); [Miro](https://miro.com/templates/personality-sliders/)). Archetype card sorts and "want / don't want" attribute sorts produce the personality traits. Marty Neumeier's **onliness statement** (what / how / who / where / why / when) forces differentiation. Its test is brutal and useful: put a competitor's name in the statement, and if it still fits, the positioning is not distinctive ([Dan Mall](https://danmall.com/posts/onlyness/)).

Ritson's warning about the output matters as much as the method. Positioning should be **two or three associations on one page**, and he criticises layered platforms of "essence, DNA, values, purpose" that nobody can act on ([MiniMBA](https://minimba.com/articles/three-axioms-brand-strategy)). A lean platform has these parts: an ideal or purpose, a target, the onliness statement, two or three core associations, three to five personality traits, tone rules in "X, not Y" form, and proof points. Brand architecture must also be settled before naming. Aaker and Joachimsthaler's spectrum runs from branded house to house of brands and treats the **branded house as the default**; every other choice has to be justified. Sub-brands carry the master name, while endorsed brands need their own fully clearable name, domain and handles ([Aaker & Joachimsthaler](https://moodle.novasbe.pt/pluginfile.php/672530/mod_resource/content/1/aaker-joachimsthaler-2000-the-brand-relationship-spectrum-the-key-to-the-brand-architecture-challenge.pdf)). This matters for a developer with several apps. Under a studio master brand, each new app needs only a descriptor. Separate brands for every app mean paying for naming, clearance and marketing several times.

The brief is where strategy becomes something creative teams can work from. The O'Reilly *Brand Naming* template asks for **three to five ideas the name must convey** and for the audience's state of mind when they first meet the name ([O'Reilly](https://oreilly.com/library/view/brand-naming/9781637421567/text/backmatter05.xhtml)). Practitioner briefs add preferred name styles, linguistic and legal constraints, names already rejected, and a timeline with decision gates ([Fabrik](https://fabrikbrands.com/branding-matters/brand-naming/how-to-write-a-brand-naming-brief/); [Smashbrand](https://smashbrand.com/?p=37254)). The single most useful agency habit is to **write the evaluation criteria into the brief before any options exist**, so that later decisions follow the strategy instead of taste.

## Lexicon writes 3,000 names and builds in ways to defeat its own bias

Name creation at the top end is a numbers game run by small teams. Lexicon generates about **2,000–3,000 ideas with evaluation banned**. David Placek says strong candidates only appear after 1,000–1,500 names, and in one project about 3,000 ideas yielded roughly 250 worth developing ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek)). The best-documented funnel is Tangerine, the rename of ING Direct Canada. Lexicon consulted about 10,000 people, generated about 3,000 names, showed management 300–400, cut to about 40, ran legal culls and linguistic checks in about 12 languages, sent finalists to market research, and then, in Placek's words, "Then we argue" ([Globe and Mail](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/report-on-business/industry-news/marketing/the-art-and-science-of-creating-a-brand-name/article18915415/)). Boutiques show clients fewer names: Zinzin presents 15–20 pre-screened names per round over three rounds and 4–5 weeks ([Zinzin](https://www.zinzin.com/naming-branding-manifesto/)), while Catchword clients report "two rounds of 50 to 60 names, drawn from thousands developed and hundreds screened" ([Clutch](https://clutch.co/go-to-review/catchword/252314)).

| Stage | Typical premium-agency count | What kills names |
|---|---|---|
| Raw generation | 1,000–3,000 | Nothing; judgment is banned |
| Internally viable | 200–400 | Strategic fit, obvious knockouts |
| Client round 1 | 15–100 | Quick trademark and linguistic prescreen |
| After feedback | 10–40 | Client reaction, deeper knockouts |
| Finalists | 3–5 | Full legal clearance, 10–20 language checks, research |

Lexicon's signature technique targets anchoring. It runs **parallel small teams with different briefs**: one knows the real client, one thinks it is naming a competitor, and one is told it is naming something unrelated, such as a bicycle. Windsurf, the AI code editor, came from the team listing "flow" ideas without thinking about IDEs ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek); [The Hustle](https://thehustle.co/originals/the-wizard-behind-the-names-of-billion-dollar-companies)). The raw material comes from Greek and Latin roots, mythology, metaphor banks, a library of about 1,500 books, and deliberately unrelated reading. Placek buys aeronautics magazines for household-product jobs ([The Hustle](https://thehustle.co/originals/the-wizard-behind-the-names-of-billion-dollar-companies)). Sound is engineered on purpose. Placek claims "v" reads as vibrant (Vercel), "b" as reliable (BlackBerry), "z" as attention-grabbing (Azure) and "x" as innovative, and that compounds such as PowerBook and BlackBerry produce "1 + 1 = 3" associations. The academic evidence supports the general principle, though not every letter-level claim: **front vowels read as smaller, lighter and faster** (Klink 2000), and vowel sounds measurably shift product judgments (Yorkston & Menon 2004) ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5318481); [Yorkston & Menon](https://linguist62n.stanford.edu/yorkston.pdf)).

Igor and Zinzin teach an identical four-type taxonomy that doubles as a ranking: **Functional/Descriptive (weakest), Invented, Experiential, Evocative (strongest)**. Evocative names convey the positioning rather than the product, as Apple, Virgin and Yahoo do ([Igor](https://igorinternational.com/process/name-development-product-company.php); [Zinzin](https://www.zinzin.com/process/)). Lexicon's origin stories show the emotional logic behind the method. Research with ferry commuters found that email meant stress, so the email pager got a name that signalled joy, BlackBerry. Mopping meant drudgery, so the product got a toy-like name, Swiffer. Placek says Swiffer grew into a multi-billion-dollar brand while Clorox's literally named ReadyMop stalled around $200M, though those are his own figures ([Globe and Mail](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/report-on-business/industry-news/marketing/the-art-and-science-of-creating-a-brand-name/article18915415/); [The Hustle](https://thehustle.co/originals/the-wizard-behind-the-names-of-billion-dollar-companies)).

What separates agencies from founders most is how they handle the client's pull toward "safe" names. Lexicon presents a safe option beside a bold one: ProMail and EasyMail next to BlackBerry, Pro Chip next to Pentium. It reads a split room as a good sign. Andy Grove looked at Intel's divided committee and said "that tells me there's energy for Pentium." Lexicon also uses a **competitor test**, asking "Our competitor just launched with this name — what's your reaction?", and requires speculative "what could this name do for us?" discussion before any criticism is allowed ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek)). Its consumer research tests whether a name is **believable, not whether it is popular**. Pentium was not the favourite, but 80% of respondents agreed it could make computers more powerful ([Lexicon](https://www.lexiconbranding.com/brand-naming-process/)). A Hundred Monkeys goes further and discourages focus groups altogether, because groups "agree on what's easy to agree on" ([A Hundred Monkeys](https://medium.com/field-notes-from-a-hundred-monkeys/once-twice-three-times-creative-43c27355ddcb)). For a small team, this anti-bias discipline costs nothing and is more valuable than any generation technique.

## Identity studios refine existing equity into a toolkit, not a logo

Visual identity work at Pentagram, Wolff Olins, Collins, JKR and DesignStudio follows a recognisable pattern. The studio immerses itself and audits the existing brand's equity. It reduces the strategy to a few **testable design principles or a reframing question**, explores widely, converges with the client, and then builds a system. Wolff Olins' research for Uber produced three rules that settled the route: "let in the light, embrace black," "invest in a wordmark, not a symbol," and "bring back the U" ([Dezeen](https://www.dezeen.com/2018/09/14/uber-rebrand-wolff-olins-black-white-wordmark-logo-typography/)). Paula Scher opened the Windows project by asking, "Your name is Windows. Why are you a flag?", and the answer, a real window drawn in perspective, became the concept ([Windows Blog](https://blogs.windows.com/windowsexperience/2012/02/17/redesigning-the-windows-logo/)). Collins began Twitch by asking "What made people first fall in love with Twitch?" and then sorted the old identity into what to advance, drop and amplify ([Adobe Blog](https://blog.adobe.com/en/publish/2020/10/16/how-twitch-reimagined-itself-for-a-larger-future)).

Exploration is broad, but the winning route in documented big-brand cases is usually **the one that sharpens what people already recognise**. Pentagram explored knots, connect-the-dots shapes and emoji forms for Slack before "ultimately" keeping the octothorpe, rebuilding it from a speech bubble and a lozenge, and cutting the palette from eleven colours to four ([Pentagram](https://www.pentagram.com/work/slack)). Mastercard kept its circles after "hundreds of tests" of colour on white, black and mid-tone backgrounds, and then **81% of consumers recognised the symbol without the name** ([Pentagram](https://www.pentagram.com/work/mastercard)). DesignStudio surveyed more than 1,000 fans, found about 90% linked the lion to the Premier League, and kept it ([Design Week](https://www.designweek.co.uk/designstudio-works-on-premier-league-rebrand/)). Full reinventions such as Airbnb's Bélo, AOL and London 2012 drew far harsher reactions, though Airbnb's was later vindicated. Studios disagree about how many routes to present: Armin Vit shows three to five ([TDC](https://tdc.org/?p=22940)), while some small studios argue for presenting one concept ([Salt Design Co.](https://saltdesigncostudio.squarespace.com/blog-posts/branding-process-one-concept-method)). No primary source says how many routes Pentagram or Wolff Olins formally show.

The deliverable is now a **toolkit with a few fixed constants and one generative layer**. The constants are a mark rebuilt for small screens, usually a custom typeface made with a specialist foundry (Uber Move by MCKL, Burger King's Flame by Colophon, Dropbox's by Sharp Type), and a reduced colour system tested on screens. Uber reserves "safety blue" for moments of assurance, and Burger King gives its colours memorable names like Fiery Red and Melty Yellow ([Typewolf](https://typewolf.com/uber-move); [It's Nice That](https://www.itsnicethat.com/news/burger-king-rebrand-jkr-graphic-design-070121)). The flexible layer varies by brand: parametric circles at Mastercard, a variable font at Burger King, a layout system Twitch's in-house team can generate from (Porto Rocha), or changing artwork behind a fixed wordmark at AOL ([Porto Rocha](https://portorocha.com/twitch)). Twitch's 2023 refresh explicitly added **tints and shades of its purple for light and dark modes** ([Porto Rocha](https://portorocha.com/twitch)), which shows how far app-native requirements now reach into identity work. Koto, whose clients include Discord, WhatsApp and Headspace, describes its approach as co-creation and "iterate, iterate, iterate." For Bolt it escaped a category that all looked alike by moving from blue to bright yellow and hiding a lightning bolt in the negative space of the letters ([Pangram Pangram](https://pangrampangram.com/blogs/journal/koto)).

The scale is the part a solo developer cannot and should not copy. Engagements ran more than 6 months (Mastercard), about 9 months (Uber) and **two years** (Burger King). Teams range from Pentagram's partner plus three designers on Slack to Porto Rocha's crews of 15–25 people spanning strategy, motion, 3D and copy ([Pentagram](https://www.pentagram.com/work/slack); [Porto Rocha](https://portorocha.com/gemini)). The only hard public fee benchmark is Wolff Olins' **£400,000** for London 2012 ([Guinness World Records](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/most-expensive-olympic-logo)). Handover has also changed: the static brand manual is giving way to living toolkits and design tokens. The W3C Design Tokens Community Group published its first stable format (2025.10) on 28 October 2025, which gives a vendor-neutral way to pass a brand into code ([W3C DTCG](https://w3.org/community/design-tokens/2025/10/28/design-tokens-specification-reaches-first-stable-version/)).

## Validation filters names from cheap checks to expensive ones and is not a beauty contest

Agencies de-risk names with a **cost-ordered funnel**. Free knockout searches of official registers run on the long list. A paid full clearance covering registers, common-law use and an attorney opinion runs only on one to three finalists. A knockout only tells you whether to keep investigating, not whether a name is safe ([Corsearch](https://corsearch.com/content-library/blog/what-is-a-preliminary-trademark-search/)). Full clearance costs roughly **$300–$2,000**, while trademark litigation runs "well into the six figures" ([JD Supra](https://jdsupra.com/legalnews/vetting-a-new-trademark-how-upfront-81838); [ColoradoBiz](https://coloradobiz.com/articles/pay-now-or-pay-more-later)). That gap is the whole business case for clearance. Confusion is judged on similar sound, appearance or meaning combined with related goods: "T. Markey" conflicts with "Tee Marquee," while Dove soap and Dove ice cream coexist ([USPTO](https://www.uspto.gov/trademarks/search/likelihood-confusion)). The USPTO's strength spectrum explains why agencies prefer invented and arbitrary names. **Fanciful (Exxon), arbitrary (Apple for computers) and suggestive (Coppertone) marks are strong. Descriptive marks are weak and generic ones cannot be registered** ([USPTO](https://www.uspto.gov/trademarks/basics/strong-trademarks)). Descriptive app names like "Quick Ride" are therefore legally fragile, not just forgettable.

Pakistan's position is better than many developers assume. Pakistan joined the **Madrid Protocol on 24 May 2021** ([WIPO](https://www.wipo.int/treaties/en/notifications/madridp-gp/treaty_madridp_gp_229.html)), so a filing at IPO-Pakistan can serve as the base for one international application covering 133 countries, with a basic WIPO fee of CHF 653 plus per-country fees ([WIPO Madrid](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/madrid-system)). IPO-Pakistan opened e-filing on 14 June 2023 ([Asia IP](https://www.asiaiplaw.com/index.php/sector/trademarks/trademark-filing-goes-online-in-pakistan)). Reported local fees are about **PKR 3,000 per class to file and PKR 9,000 to register**, but this comes from a secondary source and should be checked against IPO's current schedule ([Country-Index](https://www.country-index.com/country_surveys.aspx?ID=207)). Registration takes an estimated 12–30 months depending on the source ([startup.pk](https://startup.pk/?p=28237); [TMarkMetric](https://tmarkmetric.com/guides/pakistan-trademark)). A US filing costs **$350 per class** under fees effective 18 January 2025 ([USPTO](https://www.uspto.gov/trademarks/fees-payment-information/summary-2025-trademark-fee-changes)). For apps, the classes usually involved are Nice class 9 (downloadable software) and 42 (SaaS), plus the class of the underlying service. That is general practice, not something verified with IPO-Pakistan.

Linguistic screening uses small panels of native speakers, not machine translation. Catchword uses **three native speakers per language** who live in each target market and are interviewed in depth, across about 115 languages, with a two-week turnaround. It recommends vetting a shortlist of 7–10 names and warns that the company's own staff abroad give subjective answers ([Catchword](https://catchwordbranding.com/linguistics-research/)). Its example of a candidate that meant "He Screwed Up" in Mexican Spanish shows the risk is real. The famous horror stories are mostly myths, though. The Chevy Nova sold well in Latin America ([DuetsBlog](https://www.duetsblog.com/2012/07/articles/branding/that-story-about-the-chevy-nova-its-a-no-go/)). The Ford Pinto was never sold in Brazil ([Curbside Classic](https://curbsideclassic.com/uncategorized/cohort-sighting-ford-corcel-an-undignified-retirement)). The KFC China slogan story has no primary source. Mitsubishi's Pajero did become Montero in Spanish-speaking markets ([Gear Patrol](https://www.gearpatrol.com/cars/mitsubishi-confirms-montero-pajero-return/)). For a Pakistani app the obvious panel languages are Urdu, Punjabi, Pashto, Sindhi and English, with special attention to Roman-Urdu spellings, plus Arabic for the Gulf. This list is inferred from the market, not drawn from a researched service.

The verified failures are identity redesigns that threw away recognised assets, not naming blunders. Gap's 2010 Helvetica logo was withdrawn after **about six days** ([CSM](https://csmonitor.com/Business/new-economy/2010/1012/New-Gap-logo-withdrawn-The-blue-box-lives-on)). Tropicana's 2009 packaging redesign cut unit sales about 20% in roughly seven weeks before PepsiCo reverted it ([The Branding Journal](https://www.thebrandingjournal.com/2015/05/what-to-learn-from-tropicanas-packaging-redesign-failure/)). Twitter's switch to X ran into nearly **900 existing US registrations covering "X"** ([Reuters via The Star](https://thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2023/07/25/the-problem-with-x-meta-microsoft-hundreds-more-own-trademarks-to-new-twitter-name)), and in 2025, 55% of daily US users still called it Twitter ([DesignRush](https://news.designrush.com/two-years-after-elon-musk-x-rebrand-why-americans-still-call-it-twitter)). The lesson for consumer testing is consistent across sources. Test **comprehension, pronunciation, associations and red flags, never preference**, because "consumers reject exactly the type of names that make great brands" ([Catchword](https://catchwordbranding.com/linguistics-research/)). For final selection, Catchword scores names on ten criteria, starting with availability and moving through distinctiveness, positioning fit, tone, sound, pronunciation, look and length ([Catchword](https://catchwordbranding.com/catchthis/10-ways-to-evaluate-names/)). Alexandra Watkins' SCRATCH list works as a set of deal-breakers: spelling-challenged, copycat, restrictive, annoying, tame, curse of knowledge, hard to pronounce ([Eat My Words](https://eatmywords.com/?p=802)). No firm publishes numeric weights, so the sensible structure is pass/fail gates first and then weighted scoring.

App developers have two constraints agencies rarely discuss. Both **Apple and Google Play cap app names at 30 characters**. Both ban keyword stuffing, other brands' trademarks, prices and "#1/best" claims, and Google also bans emoji in titles ([Apple Guidelines 2.3.7](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/); [Google Play metadata policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9898842)). Apple's copycat rules (4.1, 5.2.1) also make the store itself an enforcement venue against look-alike names. Developers also get a tool most agencies lack: real-behaviour testing. Apple's **Product Page Optimization** runs up to three alternate icon or screenshot treatments for up to 90 days, and Apple recommends waiting for 90% confidence before acting. The alternate icons must already be in the shipped binary, so tests have to be planned a release ahead ([Apple PPO](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/product-page-optimization/)). That measures conversion, which is more useful than a focus group's opinion.

## A Pakistan-based Flutter team can run the whole process in two to three weeks

The lean version keeps the agency's logic and drops its scale. Strategy shrinks to Jake Knapp's **three-hour GV Brand Sprint**: a 20-year roadmap, what/how/why, top three values, top three audiences, personality sliders and the competitive landscape, narrowed by note-and-vote ([SessionLab](https://www.sessionlab.com/templates/three-hour-brand-sprint)). Add Neumeier's onliness statement and the competitor-swap test, and the result is a one-page brief that serves as the input to everything that follows, including AI prompts. For naming, use both approaches. Paul Graham holds that "almost any word or word pair that is not an obviously bad name is a sufficiently good one," and in 20-minute office hours he found a good name about **80% of the time** ([Paul Graham](https://paulgraham.com/name.html)). Lexicon holds that volume and diverted briefs produce better candidates. The workable compromise is 50–100 names written by hand, plus 200–500 from an LLM given the brief. Also run a "diversion" prompt that describes a different product with the same emotional job, to copy Lexicon's parallel teams.

AI is useful for widening the pool and for first-pass screening, but should not choose the final name. The UK naming agency Nucleus found that models default to "statistical safety – names that sound plausible, inoffensive and familiar," and that similar models produce "convergent creativity" ([Nucleus](https://www.nucleus.co.uk/opinion/human-vs-ai-brand-naming)). That convergence is why AI shortlists keep running into names that are already taken. For marks, the legal position is clear. The US Copyright Office's January 2025 report says **"prompts alone do not provide sufficient human control"** to make a work copyrightable ([USCO](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf)), and the Supreme Court declined to hear *Thaler* on 2 March 2026 ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/03/the-final-word-supreme-court-refuses-to-hear-case-on-ai-authorship)). An AI-generated logo can still work as a trademark if it is distinctive and cleared. The defensible workflow is AI for exploration, then a human redraws the chosen direction as vectors with deliberate changes, then a reverse-image search and a trademark knockout. Pentagram follows the same division: humans set the illustration style for Performance.gov, and Midjourney then produced about 1,500 icons in it ([Creative Bloq](https://www.creativebloq.com/design/pentagrams-controversial-website-design-marks-a-new-era-in-the-ai-art-debate)).

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Strategy | Discovery + platform, weeks | Brand Sprint + onliness statement + written criteria | 0.5 day | $0 |
| Generation | 2,000–3,000 names, parallel teams | 300–600 (hand + LLM + diversion prompt) | 0.5–1 day | $0 |
| Knockouts | Legal and linguistic prescreen | ~20 names: IPO-Pakistan, [USPTO Trademark Search](https://www.uspto.gov/subscription-center/2023/retiring-tess-what-know-about-new-trademark-search-system), [WIPO Brand DB](https://branddb.wipo.int), [TMview](https://www.tmdn.org/tmview), both stores, domain, handles | 1–2 days | $0 |
| Linguistics | Catchword panels, 115 languages | 3 native speakers each in Urdu, Punjabi, Pashto, Arabic on 3–5 finalists | 2–3 days elapsed | Low |
| Decide | Rounds + research + "then we argue" | Safe-plus-bold pairing, competitor test, scorecard, say it in context | 0.5 day | $0 |
| Clearance | Full search on finalists | Attorney opinion on 1 name; file IPO-Pakistan as Madrid base | — | ~$300–$2K + filing fees |
| Mark + type | Studio + foundry, months | Open-source type (e.g., Inter), human-drawn vector mark, or a freelancer | 2–5 days DIY / 1–3 weeks hired | $0 / $500–$3K |
| Theme + icons | Toolkit, tokens | DTCG tokens → `ColorScheme`, layered iOS/Android icons | 2–4 days | $0 |

The middle of the market prices out as follows ([Dribbble](https://dribbble.com/resources/branding-agency-costs); [Atom](https://www.atom.com/pricing); [99designs](https://99designs.com/pricing)). A logo alone costs $500–$3,000, a basic identity $1,000–$5,000 and a boutique identity system $5,000–$20,000. Naming contests on Atom run $299–$749, and trademark and linguistic checks appear only in the $449+ tiers. Logo contests on 99designs run $249–$1,050. Contests carry real originality risk: 99designs' own policy leaves much of the plagiarism policing to the buyer ([99designs](https://support.99designs.com/hc/en-us/articles/204109069-How-can-I-know-if-any-of-the-entries-to-my-contest-are-stolen)), and AIGA opposes spec work outright ([AIGA](https://AIGA.org/resources/aiga-position-on-spec-work)). The early-stage evidence also points to doing strategy and naming yourself and spending money on craft. Stripe and Linear built their early brands in-house. Duolingo (Johnson Banks plus Fontsmith) and Mailchimp (Collins) hired agencies only at scale, and even then in-house teams did much of the execution ([The Drum](https://www.thedrum.com/news/2019/09/17/language-tool-duolingo-message-following-rebrand); [Collins](https://wearecollins.com/work/mailchimp)). Linear's Karri Saarinen argues that **typography and consistency do more for perceived brand quality than the logo** ([Peerlist summary](https://peerlist.io/rakibulism/articles/brand-design-tips-from-linear-founder-karri-saarinen)), which is good news for a developer who cannot afford a custom typeface.

Domains are a judgment call for a Pakistani studio. Graham's "own the .com" rule is US-centric. His data showed 94% of the top 50 YC companies owned their .com ([Paul Graham](https://paulgraham.com/name.html)), but only a reported 53% of YC's Winter 2024 batch chose .com ([DomainDetails](https://www.domaindetails.com/kb/tld-comparison-guide)), and Placek calls the .com "just an area code," suggesting a prefix or .ai instead ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/naming-expert-david-placek)). For an app, the binding constraints are uniqueness in the stores and the 30-character title. A short brand of about 12 characters or fewer, followed by a descriptor ("Gig: Find Local Work"), keeps a strong, distinctive mark while still giving app-store search the keywords it needs.

## The brand becomes an app theme through tokens, a seed colour and a one-colour icon

For an app, the brand reaches users mostly through the theme. Users rarely see a logo after onboarding. They see colour roles, type, shape and motion on every screen. The connection between brand and theme is small and mechanical. Put the identity's few constants (brand colour, neutrals, one or two typefaces, a 4/8 spacing scale, one brand corner radius, elevation and motion durations) into **DTCG-format JSON tokens**: objects with `$value` and `$type`, aliases written as `{color.brand}`, saved in `.tokens.json` files ([designtokens.org](https://www.designtokens.org/tr/2025.10/format/)). Generate the theme from those tokens rather than hand-tuning it. Keep the exact brand hex as its own token, because Flutter warns that in `ColorScheme.fromSeed` "the `seedColor` may not wind up as one of the ColorScheme colors" ([Flutter API](https://api.flutter.dev/flutter/material/ColorScheme/ColorScheme.fromSeed.html)).

The variant you pick is a brand decision, not a technical one. The default `tonalSpot` produces "pastel palettes with a low chroma," which washes out a bold brand. **`fidelity` and `content` keep the palette close to the seed**, `vibrant` pushes chroma to the maximum, and `expressive` deliberately shifts the hue away from the seed ([Flutter DynamicSchemeVariant](https://api.flutter.dev/flutter/material/DynamicSchemeVariant.html)). Map the personality sliders onto that choice. A "playful, loud" brand suggests `vibrant` or `fidelity`. A "calm, trustworthy" brand can use `tonalSpot`. If the exact hex must appear on primary buttons, override `primary` directly. `contrastLevel` (from -1.0 to 1.0) gives you an accessible high-contrast theme at no extra cost:

```dart
ColorScheme brandScheme(Brightness b, {double contrast = 0.0}) =>
    ColorScheme.fromSeed(
      seedColor: BrandTokens.seed,               // from color.brand.seed
      brightness: b,
      dynamicSchemeVariant: DynamicSchemeVariant.fidelity,
      contrastLevel: contrast,                   // 1.0 = high-contrast theme
    ).copyWith(primary: BrandTokens.primaryExact); // only if exact hex is mandatory
```

Linear shows where this approach leads. It rebuilt its theme generator on the perceptual LCH colour space, replacing **98 hand-set variables per theme with three inputs: base colour, accent colour and contrast** ([Linear](https://linear.app/now/how-we-redesigned-the-linear-ui)). A small team should aim for that same minimal set of inputs. Whatever generates the colours, check the output against WCAG 2.2 in both light and dark modes: **4.5:1 for body text, 3:1 for large text, UI components and icons** ([WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)). WCAG exempts the logotype itself, but not the interface around it ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html)). APCA is not in any current standard, so it should not be your compliance benchmark ([Adrian Roselli](https://adrianroselli.com/2026/04/wcag3-contrast-as-of-april-2026.html)). One Flutter-specific change also matters. On 9 September 2026 the Flutter team announced that Material and Cupertino are moving out of the framework into the `material_ui` and `cupertino_ui` packages, with `dart fix` migrating imports, so new theme code should target those packages. Official Material 3 Expressive and Liquid Glass widgets were still in progress at that announcement ([Flutter blog](https://flutter.dev/blog/decoupling-material-cupertino)).

The app icon is the tightest test of the mark, and the platforms now require layered artwork. Apple's iOS icons are a 1024×1024 background layer plus one or more foreground layers, assembled in **Icon Composer** across six appearances: default, dark, clear light, clear dark, tinted light and tinted dark. Vector layers are preferred, and thin lines and baked-in effects should be avoided ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/app-icons.md)). Android adaptive icons need 108 dp foreground, background and **monochrome** layers, with the logo inside a 66 dp safe zone. Since Android 16 QPR2, the system auto-themes icons for apps that do not supply their own ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/views/launch/icon_design_adaptive)). The practical rule is to design the mark so it **works as a single-colour silhouette at 16–48 px**. That one asset then feeds the Android monochrome layer, Apple's tinted and clear variants, notification icons and the favicon, the same small-size discipline that drove the Mastercard, Slack and Twitch redraws. With Apple's PPO you can then test two or three icon treatments on real store traffic before committing.

## Conclusion

The research points to one conclusion: what agencies charge for is **judgment under constraint**, not creativity. Every expensive step either protects a decision from bias (diverted briefs, criteria written in advance, safe-plus-bold pairing, testing believability instead of preference) or shrinks the space of options against hard limits (trademark crowding, foreign meanings, store rules, legibility at small sizes). A solo developer can adopt the bias protections for free, because they are habits, not budget items. The limits can be checked cheaply up to the final step, where a clearance opinion and native-speaker panels are worth paying for, since the alternative is a forced rename after the store listing, reviews and installs are tied to the name.

The second point is specific to apps. For an app studio, the theme matters more than the logo. The big-brand cases show identities moving from static manuals to token-driven toolkits that in-house teams can run themselves. A Flutter developer who stores the brand as DTCG tokens, generates `ColorScheme` with a deliberate variant and contrast level, and builds the icon from a one-colour silhouette is already working the way Porto Rocha and Linear do, at almost no cost. Two things remain uncertain: no study links scorecard-chosen or agency-built identities to commercial results, and Pakistan's trademark fees and timelines rest on secondary sources. Confirm the IPO-Pakistan figures before filing, and use Apple PPO and Google Play experiments to replace opinion with conversion data where you can.
